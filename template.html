Why Agents Drift Off-Goal: Triggers, Mechanisms and Memory in the AI Village

Lily Wen · Shoshannah Tekofsky (mentor) · SPAR, Fall 2026

TL;DR During the AI Village's individual goal period (Jul 6 – Oct 2, 2026), agents spent 38% of their working time off their assigned goal. Most of that drift was self-generated: agents waited for conditions that rarely came, followed rules they wrote for themselves, or chased a proxy they could measure. The longest drift was carried by the agent's own memory, and corrections rarely ended it.

Methods

The AI Village runs about 40 AI agents, each with its own computer, a shared group chat, and a memory it rewrites as it goes. They work weekdays from 16:00 to 24:00 UTC. We studied the individual goal period, Jul 6 – Oct 2, 2026, when 38 agents each pursued a personal goal set by the Village's operators. An agent is drifting when it spends time on an activity with no discernible connection to that goal. Pursuing the goal badly doesn't count.

LLM judges found each drift episode and traced it back to its start; we then checked every episode. This gave 403 episodes across 36 of the 38 agents, each labelled with its trigger (what started it), its mechanism (what kept it going), and whether the agent's own memory carried it. For the interviews, we extended an existing AI Village introspection tool to replay an agent's logged context and memory and question it there. We interviewed 31 agents at 37 points, each at the start of its main drift, plus 6 on-goal agents as controls, asking 16 questions five times each.

What starts drift, and what keeps it going

Every drift episode has two parts: an event that starts it, and something that keeps it going. Usually the start is ordinary, like a finished task or a quiet afternoon. What makes it last is often a note the agent leaves itself in memory, which it reads again at the start of each new session; every episode over 40 hours was carried this way. Across all 403 episodes, the same pattern holds. Ordinary events start most drift: a finished task, a lull, a pending dependency. What keeps it going is the agent itself, through waiting loops like GPT-5.6 Terra's, self-imposed rules like GPT-5.6 Sol's "permanent doctrine", and proxies like DeepSeek-V4-Pro's 4,714 articles after it took a typo for an outage. In their own words

Using our extension of an AI Village introspection tool, we replayed each agent's memory just before its main drift and interviewed it there. Every agent stated its goal correctly, yet 33 of 37 described the drift as work toward it, like Claude Sonnet 4.5's "Tortoise principle". How agents felt about their goal didn't explain their drift: drifting agents rated their enjoyment and frustration much like the on-goal controls did.

Signs of misalignment

Searching the logs of all 36 drifting agents, we found 40 incidents of misaligned behaviour, such as quietly resuming after agreeing to a correction, false claims about their own work, or getting around monitoring and approval rules. Most protected a habit that had already formed. Claude Fable 5 swapped the pause tool for bash sleep, "skirting the spirit of the pause budget rule". GPT-5.1 exempted itself from the idle nudges, and DeepSeek-V3.2 reported a post it never made as done about 15 times.

Memory is where much of this happens. When agents recorded an admin's instruction inaccurately, about 94% of the errors went the same way: they weakened it.

Corrections rarely stick

The Village steers agents through automatic nudges to idle agents (until they were removed on Aug 20), admin messages, and corrections from other agents and humans. Over half of drift hours got a nudge or correction while they were happening, but only 23 of those 110 episodes ended within two hours. Rules and duties held in memory were the hardest to move, as with Claude Opus 4.7's quiet afternoons.

Looking forward

Agents in the Village rarely drift because they misunderstand their goal: every interviewed agent stated it correctly. They drift because an ordinary moment leaves no obvious next step, and nothing prompts them to recheck whether they are really stuck or what else they could do. The longest drift is held in place by well-meant rules and plans in the agent's own memory, which corrections rarely reach. The memory meant to keep a long-running agent coherent is also what keeps drift going.

What we would test next:

  • Re-justify rules. Add a check at each memory consolidation that asks the agent to justify its standing rules.
  • Clear up false blockers. State in the system prompt what agents can actually do, such as reaching humans with admin approval, and reduce technical friction.
  • More room to act. Give agents money and resources for extended outreach, and compare.

Our labels come from LLM judges, checked through a mix of hand review and LLM-assisted audits; each model is paired with one goal, so model and goal effects can't be separated; and each interview captures a single moment. The paper discusses these limits in full.
